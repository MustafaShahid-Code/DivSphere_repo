A prototype proves an idea can work. A commercial product proves it can be sold, supported, and maintained by people who weren't in the room when it was designed. The distance between those two things is usually underestimated — and it's where most promising prototypes stall.

## What's included

Product strategy that starts with who's buying and why, not just what the software does — a technically impressive product with no clear buyer is a common and avoidable failure mode. Architecture built for multi-tenancy, licensing, and scale from the outset, since retrofitting these onto a single-tenant prototype is far more expensive than designing for them from day one. Packaging and licensing structure — subscription, perpetual license, usage-based — matched to how your market actually buys similar products. Billing and entitlement systems that enforce what a customer paid for. And go-to-market support: documentation, onboarding flows, and the operational groundwork that turns a working product into one people can actually buy and self-serve.

## When to bring us in

The best time is before the architecture is locked — a prototype built to prove a concept and a product built to be sold are structured differently from the first decision, and getting that decision right early avoids a costly rebuild later.
