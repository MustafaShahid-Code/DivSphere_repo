Good design isn't decoration applied at the end of a build — it's a research and problem-solving discipline that, done properly, changes what actually gets built. Software that's technically correct but genuinely unpleasant to use loses to a simpler competitor that respects the user's time, almost every time.

## What's included

User research grounded in how people actually work, not assumptions about how they should work — the gap between the two is where most usability problems originate. Product design that solves the real underlying problem, not just the surface-level feature request that was written down. Design systems that keep a growing product visually and interactionally consistent as more people build on top of it, so consistency doesn't erode as the team scales. Prototyping and usability testing before development starts, since a design flaw caught in a prototype costs a redesign; the same flaw caught after launch costs a rebuild plus a frustrated user base already used to the broken version. And close collaboration with engineering throughout, so what ships matches what was designed, not a simplified version compromised by handoff friction.

## When to bring us in

As early as possible — ideally before development starts, since a design decision made with real user research behind it is far cheaper to build once than to build, ship, and then rebuild after users struggle with it.
